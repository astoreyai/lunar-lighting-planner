                  Why it works this way

                  The Sun skims the horizon

                  The Moon's axis tilts only 1.5°. At the pole the Sun stays within about 1.5° of the horizon. A ridge a few hundred metres high, tens of kilometres away, can block it.

                  A day lasts a month

                  Away from the poles the Sun is up for about 14.75 Earth days, then down for 14.75. A solar-powered lander must finish its work or survive the night.

                  Earth hangs almost still

                  The Moon keeps one face toward Earth, so Earth barely moves in the sky. On the far side it never rises. Near the pole it moves above and below the horizon each month.

                  No air, sharp shadows

                  The Moon has no atmosphere. There is no twilight and no bending of light over the horizon. The line between sunlit and dark ground is pure geometry.

                  Method and accuracy

                  Sun and Earth: JPL Horizons, MOON_ME frame, hourly, 1969–2040. Terrain: LOLA LDEM_85S_40M, LDEM_75S_240M and LDEM_16. The Sun counts as up while any part of it clears the terrain; Earth while its centre does; eye height 2 m.

                  Sun and Earth directions match Horizons' own sky tables to 0.0002° and 0.006°. Long-term sunlight matches the LOLA team's polar maps (r = 0.994). On the same terrain grid, the lit-terrain map agrees with the horizon tracer at 99.8% of tested points and times.
