              Why it works this way

              The Sun skims the horizon

              The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5°, so at the pole the Sun circles the sky within about 1.5° of the horizon. A ridge a few hundred metres high, tens of kilometres away, can block it.

              A day lasts a month

              Away from the poles the Sun is up for about 14.75 Earth days, then down for 14.75. Solar-powered landers must finish their work, or survive the night.

              Earth hangs almost still

              The Moon keeps one face toward Earth, so Earth barely moves in the sky. On the far side it never rises, and near the pole it wobbles on and off the horizon each month.

              No air, sharp shadows

              With no atmosphere there is no twilight and no bending of light over the horizon: the line between sunlit and dark ground is pure geometry.

              Now, on this device's clock

              Sunlight is a power input and Earth is line of sight only. This is a training and planning aid on public terrain data, not flight software: it has not been tested with crews.

              Method and accuracy

              Sun and Earth: JPL Horizons, MOON_ME frame, hourly, 1969–2040. Terrain: LOLA LDEM_85S_40M, LDEM_75S_240M and LDEM_16. The Sun counts as up while any part of it clears the terrain; Earth while its centre does; eye height 2 m.

              Sun and Earth directions match Horizons' own sky tables to 0.0002° and 0.006°. Long-term sunlight matches the LOLA team's polar maps (r = 0.994). On the same terrain grid, the lit-terrain map agrees with the horizon tracer at 99.8% of tested points and times.
